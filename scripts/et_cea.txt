SECTIONS Should India really wish for a stronger rupee as it turns a corner? ET Online Last Updated: Nov 19, 2026, 02:27:00 PM IST Rate Story Follow us Share Font Size Abc Small Abc Medium Abc Large Save Print Comment Synopsis India may face a new currency challenge in FY28 as the rupee strengthens against the US dollar, Chief Economic Adviser V Anantha Nageswaran has warned. Cheaper crude oil and stronger foreign investment inflows could boost the rupee, but appreciation may hurt exporters, IT companies and manufacturers. Here's why a stronger rupee could create fresh challenges for India's economy and the RBI. iStock A stronger rupee may benefit India but hurt exporters, IT firms and manufacturers For much of this year, the rupee's relentless slide against the dollar has been a source of economic anxiety. There were even fears that it could breach the psychologically important 100 mark. Now, as the currency stages a recovery, Chief Economic Advisor V Anantha Nageswaran has offered a rather different warning. India's challenge next year could be managing the rupee's strength rather than its weakness. Just after India began to panic over a sliding rupee, it might discover soon that a rising rupee is not necessarily an unqualified economic blessing. Also Read: India may have to manage rupee strength, not weakness, in FY28: CEA V Anantha Nageswaran What is Nageswaran worried about? Speaking at FICCI's annual general meeting yesterday, Nageswaran painted an optimistic picture of India's economic prospects for 2027-28. He pointed to the prospect of lower crude oil prices, stronger foreign capital inflows and a possible redirection of global investments away from expensive AI-related assets towards other markets. Several agencies expect Brent crude to remain below $80 a barrel next year. If that happens, India could benefit from a substantial reduction in its energy import bill, bringing down input costs and inflation while supporting economic growth. India attracted $5.2 billion in net foreign institutional investor equity inflows during July and August when concerns about AI safety emerged. Net FII inflows had reached around $10 billion in November up to the day before his speech. The combination of cheaper oil and stronger capital flows could improve India's external position and increase demand for the rupee. "Who knows, our problem next year may be one of managing rupee strength rather than rupee weakness in FY28," he said. Also Read: Bohemian Currency: We shouldn’t stress about easy come-easy go, little high-little low of the rupee His optimism comes against the backdrop of an improving export performance. India's exports of goods and services reached $568 billion in the first seven months of FY27, putting the country on course to approach the $1 trillion mark for the full year. Nageswaran also noted that exports excluding petroleum products and gems and jewellery had grown 18%. This is where the potential currency problem becomes relevant. India is trying to build on its export momentum just as the possibility of a stronger rupee is emerging. The export competitiveness problem A stronger rupee makes Indian goods and services more expensive for overseas buyers if exporters do not reduce their dollar prices. Alternatively, exporters can retain their foreign currency prices but receive fewer rupees when converting their earnings. Either way, their competitive position or profitability can come under pressure. This applies particularly for labour-intensive sectors such as textiles, leather, garments, handicrafts and agricultural products. These industries compete in international markets where price differences can influence sourcing decisions. Their margins are often relatively thin and they have less room than larger companies to absorb currency movements. India is attempting to expand its presence in precisely these sectors. Nageswaran himself identified labour-intensive manufacturing and services as important sources of competitive advantage in the coming decades. A sharp appreciation could complicate that ambition. Indian manufacturers would have to contend with competitors from countries whose currencies may not have strengthened to the same extent. The problem is not merely theoretical. ET reported in August, citing Nuvama Institutional Equities, that rupee depreciation and higher prices had helped push revenue growth among export-oriented companies to 15% in the April-June quarter, against 6% in FY26. The report warned that some of these tailwinds could fade in the second half of FY27. A sharp appreciation would add to that pressure. IT exporters could lose an earnings cushion The information technology sector offers another example of this phenomenon. Most large Indian IT services companies earn a substantial proportion of their revenues in dollars. When those earnings are converted into rupees, depreciation increases their reported revenue and can support operating margins. The reverse happens when the rupee appreciates. Companies receive fewer rupees for the same dollar revenue, potentially squeezing margins unless they improve productivity, renegotiate contracts or reduce costs. This could become an additional concern for an industry already navigating slower technology spending and uncertainty over AI-led disruption. The currency benefit should not be confused with underlying business growth. A company may report better rupee-denominated earnings because of depreciation even when its dollar revenue growth remains weak. Equally, appreciation can reduce reported earnings without any deterioration in its actual overseas business. The challenge is therefore particularly relevant when the industry is trying to protect margins while adapting to technological changes. Manufacturing is a complex story India wants to increase its share of global manufacturing and reduce its dependence on imported goods. But a stronger currency can make imported finished products cheaper relative to domestically manufactured alternatives. This can increase competitive pressure on Indian producers in their home market, particularly when domestic manufacturers have not yet achieved economies of scale or comparable productivity. It also raises questions about the effectiveness of export incentives and other measures designed to make Indian manufacturing more competitive. However, there is an important qualification here. A stronger rupee also makes imported machinery, technology, components and raw materials cheaper. This can benefit manufacturers that depend heavily on imported inputs and reduce the cost of upgrading production facilities. The net impact will therefore differ across industries. Manufacturers with substantial imported input requirements may gain, while those competing largely on labour costs and export prices could lose. The bigger concern is whether currency appreciation outpaces improvements in productivity and manufacturing efficiency. The benefits are substantial, too None of this means India should prefer a weak currency. A stronger rupee would provide considerable relief to an import-dependent economy. India imports most of its crude oil requirements. A stronger currency reduces the rupee cost of buying oil, even if international prices remain unchanged. It can also lower the cost of imported fertilisers, electronics, machinery and industrial components. These savings can feed into lower production costs and consumer prices. There are several other benefits too. Companies with unhedged dollar-denominated foreign debt would find repayments cheaper in rupee terms. Indian students studying overseas and households paying for foreign travel would also gain. A stronger currency could help moderate imported inflation, giving the RBI greater room to manage monetary policy. Indeed, Nageswaran's own optimism rests partly on the possibility of lower oil prices delivering precisely these benefits. The point is that the economic gains from appreciation must be weighed against its potential impact on exporters. The RBI's balancing act The recent recovery of the rupee also needs to be seen in context. Goldman Sachs believes the worst phase of depreciation is behind the currency. Goldman Sachs expects the rupee to trade at around 94 against the dollar over the next three months, before gradually weakening to 96 over the following 12 months. This suggests stabilisation rather than an uninterrupted appreciation. The recovery has been helped by foreign inflows and RBI measures to curb speculative dollar demand. Tighter restrictions on currency derivative positions had reduced pressure on the rupee. Foreign investors returned to Indian bonds in November as falling oil prices and currency stability improved the outlook. But the RBI will have to be careful about allowing either excessive depreciation or an abrupt appreciation. It has reason to resist disorderly movements in both directions, particularly when they disrupt business planning and currency hedging. Competitiveness matters more than the exchange rate Ultimately, India's objective should not be to defend a particular rupee-dollar level. A weak currency cannot compensate indefinitely for inadequate productivity, expensive logistics, poor infrastructure or uncompetitive manufacturing costs. Equally, a strong currency should not become a handicap for businesses trying to compete globally. Nageswaran's larger argument at FICCI was that the post-pandemic world will be shaped by supply-side disruptions and greater geopolitical uncertainty. India needs to build resilience while making itself indispensable to global supply chains. That will require more competitive manufacturing, better skills and greater value addition. A strong rupee would be welcome if it reflects these underlying improvements. But if it starts eroding export competitiveness before Indian industry has become sufficiently productive, the benefits could come at a cost. The real aspiration, therefore, should not be a super strong rupee. It should be an economy strong enough to remain competitive regardless of where the currency trades.
